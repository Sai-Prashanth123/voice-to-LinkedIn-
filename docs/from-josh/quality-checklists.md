# Quality checklists

Attachment to *Reference library inputs* v1.0. Companion to the content system build spec v2.0.
Josh Fryszer to Thought Pilot, 4 September 2026.

Four checklists a draft can be tested against. Offered as raw material for the gate in clause 9.6,
not as a specification. Use what is useful.

These sit under the behaviour clauses 9.7 and 9.8 already fix, which matters more than any check below.

---

## 1. The master test

The one that sits above the others. Pass or fail, no score.

> **Could someone else have written this?**

If yes, it fails. It is generic and has no fingerprint on it.
If no, it passes, because only I was in that room and had that conversation.

Run it on the hook and on the central claim. That is where generic writing shows up first.

### What a fail looks like

- "Most sales teams are bad at prospecting." No specificity, no story, no experience behind it.
- "Consistency is the key to outbound." Generic claim. Anyone could write it.
- "I have learned a lot about targeting over the years." Vague. No moment, no person, no detail.

### What a pass looks like

- "Last week I sat with a VP of Sales who had four SDRs and a Clay build nobody had opened in
  two months." Only one person was in that room.
- "In 2024 I turned down a piece of work worth more than a quarter of my revenue." Specific year,
  specific stake, specific decision.

### Depth, when it passes

Not all passes are equal, and the gate should record which one it got.

| Depth | What it is | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | A specific scene. Time, person, detail. | Strongest |
| 2 | Time-anchored. A real period, not a scene. | Strong |
| 3 | Earned perspective across years, with the track record behind it. | Weakest pass. Still a pass. |

### If it fails

One question back, not a list. Something like: is there a specific moment where you actually
experienced this? Who is the real person at the centre of it? What is the number or the date that
makes this unmistakably yours?

## 2. Will they keep reading

Six criteria. Each gets a verdict and one sentence of reasoning. The output ends with a single
priority fix, not six things to do.

**1. Is there a human in it?** A real person in a real moment, doing or feeling something. Not
"many teams", not "clients often". A specific person, even anonymised.

**2. Is it thought leadership or just content?** Content gives information. Anyone can write it.
Thought leadership changes how the reader sees something. Does the post challenge an assumption, or
does it confirm what they already knew?

**3. Is it a lesson or just advice?** Advice is telling. A lesson is showing. Was the insight earned
by doing the thing, or could it have been written by someone who never has?

**4. Does the formatting help?** Can it be skimmed in ten seconds and still reward three minutes?
Or is it a wall of text, or a bullet list standing in for real sentences?

**5. Does the reader know early why they should care?** The context and the stakes should land in
the first few lines, before the insight arrives.

**6. Would I read every word if a stranger wrote it?** The honest gut check.

---

## 3. Formatting

Five elements. Same output shape: verdicts, then one priority fix.

**Lean, fat, lean.** Short and punchy to get in, the depth in the middle, a short clear exit. If the
opening is heavy, the reader never reaches the middle.

**Sentence length variation.** Everything the same length is exhausting. Short lines create emphasis,
longer ones carry the detail. Read it aloud: rhythm or legal document?

**Colons.** The most underused tool available. A colon creates a micro-pause and a micro-curiosity at
once, and pulls the eye to the next line.

**Bullet discipline.** Bullets surface a genuine list. They are a crutch when they replace sentences
and fragment ideas that should flow. One list per post is usually plenty. If the bullets could be a
sentence, they should be.

**White space.** One idea per paragraph. When the idea shifts, break. Four or five lines already
reads as dense on a phone.

## 4. Is it actually saying anything

A four-level scale. Name the level, then ask one question that lifts it.

| Level | What it is |
|---|---|
| 1 | Tips anyone could write. No experience, no point of view. Useful and forgettable. |
| 2 | Useful tips backed by real experience. Better, still not distinctive. |
| 3 | A clear opinion or perspective. The reader knows who wrote it. |
| 4 | Challenges an assumption and shifts how the reader sees the thing. Only I could have written it. |

### The questions that lift a post

One at a time, never a list.

- What do most people in this field believe about this, and where do you disagree?
- When did you actually learn this? What happened that proved it?
- What surprised you most when you hit this in real life?
- What is the uncomfortable version of this that most people do not say out loud?
- Who is the conventional wisdom actually wrong for, and why?
- What did you used to believe about this that you no longer believe?

The point is to find what I actually think. Manufacturing disagreement produces a different kind of post
and a worse one.

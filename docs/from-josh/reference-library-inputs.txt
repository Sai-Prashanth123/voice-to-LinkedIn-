# The half of the spec I owe you
 The spec left one section deliberately open on the basis that I would bring my half of
 it. This is that half. How I want to write, who I am writing for, and three files of raw material to
 go with it. Where you already have a piece of this solved, your version wins.

 

 

 
 1How to read this

 

## Material, not instructions

 Section 8 of the spec says, in effect, that I bring the pillars, the body frameworks, the hook rules,
 the closing lines, the questions the system asks me, and who I am talking to. This is that, and it has
 been sitting unanswered since August.

 It came out of four separate bodies of work by four different people who contradict each other in
 places. You should not have to referee that, so I have made the calls and written it up as one settled
 set of rules rather than a menu.

 
 The part that matters most
 You have built systems like this before and I have not. If any of what follows is already covered by
 how you build, or you have a better way, say so and skip it. I would rather use your method than
 mine. I am trying to protect the philosophy, not the mechanics.

 

 The only thing not open to a better idea is the test in clause 1: could anyone else have written this.
 Everything else here is a preference and I will happily be talked out of it.

 

 

 
 2The audience

 

## A more sophisticated reader than most advice assumes

 Nearly all LinkedIn writing advice is built for solo operators, freelancers and early-stage founders.
 My buyer is not that, and this is the single biggest adjustment I want the system to make. Every rule
 below sits underneath this one.

 
 
 | | Who they are | Detail

 
 | The person | A VP of Business Development, a VP of Sales, or a head of inside sales. That level.

 | The company | A vertical SaaS business, Series A through Series C.

 | The motion | An existing outbound motion. Nobody is starting from zero.

 | The team | At least five SDRs, BDRs or account executives doing the outreach.

 | Who they sell to | SMB and mid-market.

 | The pain | Not booking the meetings they want. Volume is not the problem. Quality and prioritisation are.

 
 
 

 
 Two words that get misread
 Sophisticated means their operating vocabulary at the altitude they work at. The
 writing stays plain, and jargon still fails. Strategic means altitude, not
 vagueness: a strategic post still carries a specific number, a specific person and a specific
 Tuesday. A post for this buyer talks about how a segment gets chosen. A post for a beginner talks
 about which button to press.

 

 

 

 
 3Pillars

 

## Where the material comes from

 Three pillars. They describe where a moment came from, not what it is about. This is the value that
 goes in the pillar field in clause 6.

 
 
 Pillar 1
 

### Career and business
 Running the consultancy. Client work, decisions, pricing, what I turned down, what went wrong,
 what I noticed across engagements. The commercial side rather than the craft.

 

 
 Pillar 2
 

### Expertise and craft
 The method itself. Finding pain in public data, building segments, what breaks in a build, what a
 data source turned out to be worth, where I was wrong about a technique.

 

 
 Pillar 3
 

### Life
 Anything outside work. It stays in, because it is where the lived experience test is easiest to
 pass. But it carries a condition, below.

 

 

 
 The condition on the life pillar
 Every life post has to land somewhere my buyer recognises, at the same altitude as everything else.
 The walk is not the post. What I worked out on the walk is. A life post with no landing attracts the
 wrong readers, and the wrong readers are worse than no readers.

 

 

 

 
 4The hook

 

## One test, one default, and a toolkit

 The test, which is pass or fail. Two things, both required. Only I could have written
 it, because only I was in that room. And it opens a loop rather than closing one. Already the first
 check in clause 9.6, and it does not move.

 What a good one usually has. Five elements in the opening lines: timeline, location,
 character, action, stakes. The stakes are the one most often left out and the one that does the most
 work, because without them the reader assumes everything was fine.

 Not every hook needs all five, and I do not want this applied mechanically. It is what we are aiming
 at rather than a template to fill. Use your judgement on when a hook lands better without one of
 them.

 
 Worked example
 In March I sat on a call with a VP of Sales in Melbourne who had just been handed a number nobody
 had worked out how to hit.

 Timeline: in March. Location: a call, Melbourne. Character: a VP of Sales. Action:
 handed a number. Stakes: nobody knew how to hit it.

 

 The toolkit, in order of how often I want it reached for.

 
 
 | | Tool | What it does | When

 
 | Name who it is for | States the job title, team size, company type or a qualifier in the opening line. | Highest value tool I have. Does the altitude work on its own, turning the right reader on and the wrong one off.

 | Contrast | Two short lines that fight each other. Past against present, expectation against reality, consensus against what I found. | Whenever the moment has a genuine before and after. Do not manufacture one.

 | Authority markers | Time, status, money or recency attached to an otherwise ordinary opening. | Only when the number is real and mine. A borrowed or rounded number is worse than none.

 | Premise, setup, trigger | A longer three-part opener. A premise with a real number, a setup that breaks the expectation, a line that teases the reveal. | Posts with a big result and a twist underneath it. Uses more of the space above the fold.

 | How I, not how to | Puts me inside the claim instead of standing outside teaching it. | Any time I actually did the thing. If I did not, the post should not exist.

 | Two stacked gaps | Line two refuses to answer line one and adds a second question instead of resolving the first. | Short hooks where there is no scene to set. Easy to overdo.

 
 
 

 Never open with a question. It reads as an advert and trips the reader's filter
 before anything has been earned. A question can close a hook, which is a different thing and often
 works well. It cannot start one.

 No bait. Some of the material I collected teaches deliberately provoking a reaction
 to lift reach. It is effective and I do not want it. It contradicts how I sell and it attracts an
 audience I cannot do anything with. If a hook only works because it annoys someone, it fails.

 

 

 
 5The body

 

## Three frameworks, and nothing else

 Clause 9.3 asks the drafter to record which framework it chose. These are the three it chooses from.

 
 
 Framework 1
 

### What, why, how
 State what happened. Explain why it happened. Show how the reader could apply it. The most
 versatile of the three and the right default when nothing else obviously fits.

 

 
 Framework 2
 

### Problem, agitate, solve
 Name a problem the reader recognises, make them feel what it costs, then show what fixed it. For
 posts about a mistake, a frustration or something broken.

 

 
 Framework 3
 

### Hook, frame, insight, lesson
 The frame is the extra step: name what most people believe before delivering what I found
 instead. For posts that challenge an assumption rather than teach a method.

 

 

 On formatting: lean, then fat, then lean. Colons pull the eye down the page and are underused. Short
 paragraphs with a break between each, because this is read on a phone. Bullets are for surfacing a
 genuine list, not for avoiding sentences, and one list per post is usually plenty. Vary the sentence
 length. No fixed word count: the moment decides the length, and nothing gets padded to reach a target.

 

 

 
 6Closing lines

 

## One ask at most, or none

 
 
 Close 1
 

### A genuine question
 Specific enough that only my reader could answer it, and one I actually want the answer to. Not
 "what do you think". The default close.

 

 
 Close 2
 

### A soft close, no ask
 A line that closes the loop the post opened and leaves the reader with something. Right for the
 more personal posts, where an ask would jar.

 

 
 Close 3
 

### One direct ask
 Message me, here is the link, let's talk. Permitted, but rationed to roughly one post in five.
 More often and the whole feed reads as a pitch.

 

 

 Never two asks in one post, because the reader then does neither. Never a request for likes, reposts
 or follows. Never a pitch bolted onto a post that was not about the offer, which reads as a bait and
 switch after someone has given you three minutes.

 

 

 
 7Proof

 

## Show it, but never name them

 There is a real tension here, worth stating rather than letting the system discover it. Writing for
 this buyer means showing proof rather than asserting expertise, and proof usually wants a named client
 and a real number. Clause 9c forbids naming anyone without express permission for that specific post.

 The resolution is that no client gets named. Not on a roster, not with permission, not at all. Posts
 use the shape instead: a vertical SaaS company, a compliance platform, a team of forty, a health tech
 business selling into clinics.

 
 This needs to be an actual check, not a hope
 Removing the name does not anonymise anything on its own. A number plus a niche plus a date
 identifies a company to anyone in that market, and my readers are in that market. Clause 9.13 already
 says this. It needs to be something the gate genuinely tests, because it is the failure mode that
 costs me a client rather than a post.

 

 Where a moment cannot survive without identifying someone, two options in order: use my own numbers
 instead of theirs, or park it. Parking it is a correct outcome.

 
 A side note on showing it
 Where a post makes a claim I can actually show, a screenshot or an image usually proves it better
 than another sentence does. A result, a dashboard, a message thread, a diagram. Worth flagging to me
 when a draft is making a claim that would land harder with something to look at. The naming rule
 above applies to anything visible in the image too.

 Everything the system asks me. Four sets: the six questions that mine a single moment plus the

 Raw material for the gate, not a specification. Clause 9.6.

 Ten single-purpose assistants, one per job: find the story, find something to write about, build

 9Over to you

 

## Two things I am leaving with you

 Before the two specifics, the general version. Ghostwriting and copywriting are your craft and not
 mine. What I have written across this document is a skeleton. If there is meat you would add to it,
 add it, and tell me where you have. That applies to everything above as much as to the two below.

 

### Screening for writing that sounds machine-generated

 Do you already screen for this? I assume so, since it is the obvious failure mode of a system like
 this, which is why I have not written it up as a requirement. I do hold a long list of patterns I treat
 as tells: certain structural cliches, filler phrases, punctuation habits, uniform sentence rhythm, a
 missing first person. If your system does not already cover it, say the word and I will send the list.
 If it does, use yours.

 

### How much weight the two feedback signals get

 Clause 12a makes engagement numbers automatic and required. Clause 12b makes the question about
 whether a post started a conversation best effort, because it depends on me turning up. How they get
 weighted against each other is your call, not mine.

 
 The one risk worth naming
 Engagement numbers arrive reliably every week. Conversations almost never do, and never on a
 schedule. So the loop will always hold far more of the weak signal than the strong one. Tuned mainly
 on numbers, it drifts toward whatever wins likes, which in my market is a style aimed at people who
 will never buy anything. My buyer lurks, reads, and does not engage. A design problem rather than an
 objection, and you will solve it better than I would.

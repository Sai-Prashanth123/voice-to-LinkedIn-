01

What you asked for

Clauses 1–2

A system that turns things you already say — voice notes, calls, working sessions — into LinkedIn posts that could only have been written by you. Three rules were never negotiable. Each is now enforced by the database rather than by our good intentions, which means they hold even if the code above them is wrong.

The lived experience test

clause 1Enforced

Could anyone else have written this? If yes, it fails. Held in a locked section of your reference library. The learning loop can propose changes to anything else and is structurally unable to touch this — a database rule rejects the attempt.

No fabrication

9.4Enforced

Every claim, quote, number and name must trace to something you actually said. The writer emits each claim with the exact source text beside it, and that is checked mechanically, before any model gives an opinion. Nothing invented survives.

Nothing publishes without you

11.2Enforced

Four database constraints make an unauthorised published post impossible to store, not merely unlikely. Tested adversarially: the database refuses a published post you never marked ready, one with no date, and one published before you approved it.

02

What is built

The nine steps

The whole pipeline exists end to end. Proven means it has been driven against the live system and watched working, not read in the code. Unproven means it is built and waits on something outside our control.

Five ways in

clause 4Proven

Voice notes, prompted sessions, call transcripts, Claude Code sessions and typed thoughts, all landing in one idea bank. Send a voice note from your phone and it is transcribed, filed and answered with a question — in seconds, between other things. 9 inputs captured so far.

The interview

clause 5Proven

Asks until a moment has a scene, a detail and a realisation — then stops. It budgets its questions, rewords prompts that never land, and parks a moment rather than padding it. It never writes; that is a different job with a different prompt.

The idea bank

clause 6Proven

28 tables, 73 access rules, and no delete permission anywhere — nothing can be destroyed, only killed and labelled. Moments re-open when you add to them. Editable by you without a developer, and exportable in full at any moment.

Choosing what gets written

clause 7Proven

Five-factor scoring, pillar balance, and a retelling check against everything already published. When it cannot fill the queue without dropping the bar it runs short and says why, because fewer posts is the right answer when the material is not there. 45 selection runs.

Drafting and the gate

clause 9Built, unproven

Eight independent checks, each able to reject on its own — because a model asked to weigh eight things at once averages across them and lets marginal work through. 130 checks have run and 48 rejected. The gate demonstrably rejects. What it has never done is pass a draft, and section 4 explains exactly why.

Visuals

clause 10Built, unproven

Send a diagram and it rebuilds the idea in your brand — drawn from scratch as a vector image, so it is structurally incapable of resembling the original rather than merely instructed not to. Your image is stored and queued; no rebuild has run yet.

The calendar

clause 11Proven

Drafts arrive as drafts. You edit in place and mark ready, and only that authorises publishing. Your edits are read back into the bank automatically, because the calendar is the bank rather than a copy of it.

The learning loop

clause 12Proven

The difference between what was written and what you approved is measured the moment you approve — it needs no LinkedIn and no effort from you. A one-line verdict is asked on the taps you already make. Changes to the library are proposed with evidence, approved only by you, and reversible.

Operations

clause 13Proven

Silence alerts, queue warnings, errors surfaced once rather than every morning, and a monthly report on the system itself. It also tells you if it starts using a service you were never told about. Nine schedules run without anyone starting them.

03

The pieces, and where they run

Four deliverables

Four things were built. Two you use directly, one runs on your machine, and one is the engine underneath.

The Telegram bot

Live

Where the system is used. Capture, the interview, the weekly pass, approving posts, clearing names, sending images, adding a rule. 15 commands and 19 buttons, all on your phone. Every decision the system needs from you can be made with a tap; nothing requires a laptop.

The desk — the web app

Live

Where prose gets edited, which a chat box cannot do. Five screens: this week, the calendar, the idea bank, the reference library, and change proposals — plus a one-click export of everything. Deployed and reachable. Sign-in is restricted to a single address, and the door is locked by default rather than open by default.

The Claude Code agent and installer

Built, not installed

Reads your working sessions on your own machine and sends a digest of what you said and decided — never code, never client material. One setup command puts it on a schedule (Windows, macOS and Linux all supported) and it never needs attention again. It has not been installed yet, so this input has produced nothing.

The engine

Live

10 background services and 26 database migrations, on nine schedules — the queue every minute, selection every four hours, publishing every five minutes, the daily check at 8am, the learning run on Monday mornings. 285 model calls made so far, at a total cost of $0.09.

Also delivered

Six written documents, because clause 14 requires them and because you should not need us to run this: how the spec was read clause by clause, the seven build decisions with the reasoning behind each, the running cost, the operating runbook, the account handover sequence, and the running order for the recorded walkthrough. Plus 84 automated tests and four evaluation harnesses that measure the system against its own acceptance criteria.

The one thing that unblocks the most

A paid model key. The build has been running on free tiers, which allow 8,000 words a minute where a single quality check needs roughly 16,000. That one fact blocks four component tests and the finish line itself.

It is worth being precise about what this is not: the gate is not broken. Reading its rejections shows it correctly catching genuine invention — phrases the free model added that you never said. The safety mechanism works; the writer beneath it is the cheap one. This is already inside the $53 a month confirmed in the cost document.

05

What we need from you

The critical path

Seven of the fourteen reference library sections are waiting on you, and they are the ones that cannot be guessed without putting words in your mouth.

The voice guide

8.1, 8.2Blocking

A recording of you talking at length, in your own words — never your old posts, which you have said have drifted. The entire quality argument rests on this, and it has no date against it. The system has already written you a first draft to react to, which is a far easier thing to answer than a blank page. It needs about 400 words of you speaking first.

Pillars, audience, gate rules, visual brand, reference posts

clause 8Waiting

Each section explains what belongs in it and why we did not draft it for you. Editable from The desk in a browser, or from Telegram with a single message — a rule can be added in under a minute, because that is how it will actually get maintained.

Connect LinkedIn

clause 11, 12.1Waiting

Posting is self-serve and needs no approval. Post analytics sits behind a review that takes time, so it is worth starting early. This begins the four-week clocks on two component tests.

Answer what is already asked, and keep sending

clauses 4, 5Waiting

Five moments are sitting asked and unanswered. Beyond those: nineteen more voice notes, two more sessions, and a decision on which call recorder you use.

The honest constraint

The critical path is calendar time, not code. Five of the twelve tests need two to four weeks of live running, and those clocks have not started. No amount of building shortens them. The most valuable thing anyone can do this week is start one.

06

Ownership, cost and handover

Clauses 14–15

It becomes yours

All code, prompts, configuration and documentation transfer to you on payment, with no ongoing licence from us. Every third-party component is permissively licensed and listed by name, so "no licence required" is checkable rather than asserted.

It runs without us

Nothing in the design depends on Thought Pilot. The accounts currently do, which is the one clause not yet met — everything runs today in our Supabase organisation, on our keys. The move into your accounts has been rehearsed rather than described: all 26 migrations were applied to an empty database to prove the sequence works.

What it costs

$45–70 a month, typically $53 — against the roughly $50 you expected. Confirmed in writing before the build, itemised line by line, with three levers if you want it firmly under $50.

Today it is running at close to zero, on free tiers, which is exactly why the gate has not been cleared. Two decisions cost money and we would not economise on either: the eight-check gate at about $6, and database backups at $25.

You will be told before a bill

The system now watches which services it actually uses and tells you in the daily message the first time it touches one that is not on your list.

Two things worth raising directly

Two services were added mid-build without telling you. Groq and Hugging Face have been running the writer, the gate and the interview on free tiers while the paid key was not configured, and the tool list you hold named neither. That was a defensible decision made silently, which is not good enough. Both are now on the list, and the system was changed so it cannot happen quietly again.

Sign-in was open when it should have been closed. The single-address restriction had a fault that let anyone signed in reach the app if the setting was missing — and the setting had never been filled in. Found while deploying, fixed before the app went public, and no real client material has ever been in the system. Development keys will be rotated before any is.
